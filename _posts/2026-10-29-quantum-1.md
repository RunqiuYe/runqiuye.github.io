## Basics of quantum computation

### Qubits

Bits are the basic units of classical computation, and correspondingly
**qubits** are their quantum counterparts. A classical bit can be either
be 0 or 1, but a quantum bit can be in a state of “superposition”. When
it is measured, there is some probability of observing 0 and some
probability of observing 1. We can think of this as a coin flip
represented by a vector $v = (v_1, v_2) \in \mathbb{R}^2$, where $v_1$ and $v_2$
are the probabilities of the 0 and 1 outcomes, respectively. For example,
the vector $(0.6, 0.4)$ gives a probability of 0.6 for observing 0 and a
probability of 0.4 for observing 1.

In quantum mechanics, however, the state of a qubit is represented by a
vector $v = (v_1, v_2) \in \mathbb{C}^2$, where $v_i$ is called the
**amplitude** of outcome $i$. For example, in the vector $( \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}, \frac{i}{\sqrt{2}} )$, $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$ is the
amplitude of the 0 outcome, and $\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}$ is the amplitude of
the 1 outcome.

The measurement axiom of quantum mechanics, called the **Born rule**,
says that the probability of observing a particular outcome is the squared
magnitude of its amplitude. That is, if the state is represented by $v = (v_1, v_2) \in \mathbb{C}^2$, then $\left\lvert v_1\right\rvert^2$ is the probability of observing
0, and $\left\lvert v_2\right\rvert^2$ is the probability of observing 1. Of course, we must
have $\left\lvert v_1\right\rvert^2 + \left\lvert v_2\right\rvert^2 = 1$. In the example above, the
probability of observing each outcome is $\frac{1}{2}$.

We can generalize to a quantum state with $d$ outcomes, which is
represented by a complex vector $v \in \mathbb{C}^d$. Again, $\left\lvert v_i\right\rvert^2$ is the
probability that a measurement produces outcome $i$. For this to be a
valid quantum state and for the probabilities to make sense, we must have

$$
\sum_{i=1}^d \left\lvert v_i\right\rvert^2 = 1.
$$

Equivalently, $\left\lVert v\right\rVert_2 = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^d \left\lvert v_i\right\rvert^2} = 1$, where
$\left\lVert \cdot\right\rVert_2$ is the $\ell_2$-norm on $\mathbb{C}^d$.

### Unitary matrices

Because the set of valid quantum states must have unit $\ell_2$-norm, the
set of viable quantum operations must preserve the $\ell_2$-norm of the
state, i.e. they must be isometries. An axiom of quantum mechanics dictates that
quantum operations must also be **linear**. Recall that
norm-preserving linear maps on $\mathbb{C}^d$ are exactly characterized by
**unitary matrices**. That is, matrices $U \in \mathbb{C}^{d \times d}$ such
that $U U^{\ast} = I$, where $U^{\ast}$ is the conjugate transpose of $U$.

### Multi-qubit quantum computation

After understanding single qubits, we would like to understand a quantum
system with multiple qubits, so we can build more complicated operations
and understand what it means to locally apply some quantum operation.

As a classical analogy, think of two coins $A = (0.6, 0.4)$ and $B = (0.7, 0.3)$. There are four possible states we can observe when flipping them,
namely 00, 01, 10, and 11. We can similarly use a vector in $\mathbb{R}^4$ to
represent their respective probabilities, namely $(0.42, 0.18, 0.28, 0.12)$.

Combining two independent quantum states works in exactly the same way,
and the machinery we use to join them is called the **tensor
product**. If we have $v \in \mathbb{C}^a$ and $w \in \mathbb{C}^b$ representing the
quantum states, the joint system is described by the tensor product $v \otimes w \in \mathbb{C}^{ab}$, where $(v \otimes w)_{ij} = v_i w_j$. We can
verify that this still has unit $\ell_2$-norm. Now, we can see that if we have
an $n$-qubit state, there will be $2^n$ possible outcomes, and it is
characterized by a $2^n$-dimensional vector.

Correspondingly, for quantum operations, we have **tensor products of
matrices**. The tensor product of matrices is the unique operator which
represents the tensor product of the underlying states. That is, for
unitary $U \in \mathbb{C}^{a \times a}$ and $V \in \mathbb{C}^{b \times b}$, the tensor
product $U \otimes V$ is defined as the unique linear operator such that

$$
(U \otimes V) (v \otimes w) = (U v) \otimes (V w)
$$

for all $v \in \mathbb{C}^a$ and $w \in \mathbb{C}^b$. The intuition is that if we apply
a unitary to a specific qubit, it should not affect any other qubit.

### Dirac notation and inner products

To express quantum states, we use Dirac notation. The most basic
notational idea is that we will use a “ket”, which looks like
$\left\lvert \cdot\right\rangle$, to describe a vector that is supposed to be a quantum
state. For example, to express the 0-outcome and 1-outcome states, we have

$$
\left\lvert 0\right\rangle = \begin{bmatrix}
  1 \\ 0
\end{bmatrix}, \quad
\left\lvert 1\right\rangle = \begin{bmatrix}
  0 \\ 1
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Now any single qubit state $\left\lvert \psi\right\rangle$ can be written as $\left\lvert \psi\right\rangle = \alpha \left\lvert 0\right\rangle + \beta \left\lvert 1\right\rangle$. To express multi-qubit states, we
write for any $x \in \lbrace 0, 1 \rbrace^n$:

$$
\left\lvert x\right\rangle = \left\lvert x_1\right\rangle \otimes \dots \otimes \left\lvert x_n\right\rangle.
$$

These are called **classical basis states**. Indeed, they form a
basis in which to express any $n$-qubit state

$$
\left\lvert \psi\right\rangle = \sum_{x \in \lbrace0, 1\rbrace^n} \alpha_x \left\lvert x\right\rangle.
$$

Other than tensor product, we also have the familiar inner product. In
Dirac notation, we use a “bra” to express the conjugate transpose, and
the inner product of two states $\left\lvert \psi\right\rangle, \left\lvert \varphi\right\rangle \in \mathbb{C}^d$ is
written as

$$
\left\langle \psi \mid \varphi \right\rangle = \sum_{i=1}^d \psi_i^{\ast} \varphi_i.
$$

Their outer product $\left\lvert \varphi\right\rangle \left\langle \psi \right\rvert$ is given by $(\left\lvert \varphi\right\rangle \left\langle \psi \right\rvert)_{ij} = \varphi_i \psi_j^{\ast}$. Now, an arbitrary $n$-qubit
unitary $U$ can be characterized by

$$
U  = \sum_{x, y \in \lbrace0, 1\rbrace^n} u_{x, y} \left\lvert x\right\rangle \left\langle y \right\rvert,
$$

where $u_{x, y} = \left\langle x \right\rvert U \left\lvert y\right\rangle$. The corresponding linear-algebra
concepts are straightforward, so we leave the proof as an
exercise.

### Summary — Quantum computation over $n$ qubits

As a brief summary, for a quantum computation over $n$ qubits, the
**states** are represented as $\left\lvert \psi\right\rangle \in \mathbb{C}^{2^n}$ such that
$\left\lVert \psi\right\rVert_2^2 = \sum_{x \in \lbrace0, 1\rbrace^n} \left\lvert \left\langle x \mid \psi \right\rangle\right\rvert^2= 1$.
An **operation** is a matrix $U \in \mathbb{C}^{2^n \times 2^n}$ with $U^{\ast} U = U U^{\ast} = I$ (i.e. unitary). Applying $U$ to $\left\lvert \psi\right\rangle$ results in the
state $U \left\lvert \psi\right\rangle$. Finally, a **measurement** causes the state to
collapse to a specific $\left\lvert x\right\rangle$ with probability $\left\lvert \left\langle x \mid \psi \right\rangle\right\rvert^2$.

### Mixed state

For the final topic of this section, we consider mixed states. The quantum
states $\left\lvert \psi\right\rangle$ we have defined previously are called **pure
states**. We say a state is **mixed** when it represents a probability
distribution of pure states.

To see the difference between pure states and mixed states, consider the
following example. Consider the pure state $\left\lvert +\right\rangle := \frac{\left\lvert 0\right\rangle + \left\lvert 1\right\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}$, and consider a mixed state which is either
$\left\lvert 0\right\rangle$ or $\left\lvert 1\right\rangle$ with $50\%$ probability. Also consider the unitary
matrix $H$, which we will later see to be the **Hadamard gate**,
defined via

$$
H = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{bmatrix}
  1 & 1  \\
  1 & -1
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Then for the pure state we have $H \left\lvert +\right\rangle = \left\lvert 0\right\rangle $, which is
guaranteed, but for the mixed state we have $H \left\lvert 0\right\rangle = \left\lvert +\right\rangle= \frac{\left\lvert 0\right\rangle + \left\lvert 1\right\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}$ and $H \left\lvert 1\right\rangle = \left\lvert -\right\rangle = \frac{\left\lvert 0\right\rangle - \left\lvert 1\right\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}$, where each happens with $50\%$
probability. In particular, for the mixed state, the chance of measuring
$\left\lvert 0\right\rangle$ is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{P} [\text{measure $\left\lvert 0\right\rangle$}]
 & = \mathbb{P} [\text{original state is $\left\lvert 0\right\rangle$}]
\cdot \mathbb{P} [\text{measure $\left\lvert 0\right\rangle$ on $\left\lvert +\right\rangle$}]  \\
 & \quad + \mathbb{P} [\text{original state is $\left\lvert 1\right\rangle$}]
\cdot \mathbb{P} [\text{measure $\left\lvert 0\right\rangle$ on $\left\lvert -\right\rangle$}]  \\
 & = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{2} +
\frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

To describe a statistical mixture of quantum states $\lbrace \left\lvert \psi_i\right\rangle \rbrace_i$, each with probability $p_i$, we can use a **density matrix**:

$$
\rho = \sum_i p_i \left\lvert \psi_i\right\rangle \left\langle \psi_i \right\rvert \in \mathbb{C}^{2^n \times 2^n},
$$

where $\sum_i p_i = 1$. As it turns out, $\rho$ is a valid density
matrix if and only if $\rho$ is a trace-$1$ positive semidefinite
matrix.

## Computation with quantum circuits

Recall that in classical computation, computation is performed through
Boolean circuits and gates. In quantum computation, we will use quantum
circuits with quantum gates. More concretely, a $n$-qubit quantum circuit
is a collection of unitary operations $G_1, \dots, G_m$ called gates,
applied in sequence to a subset of $n$ wires. The composition of the gates
in the circuit generates a $2^n \times 2^n$ unitary operation. We assume
that each gate is tensored with the identity operation on each
wire that it does not touch. Consider the following example with $3$
qubits and $3$ gates:

As shown in the figure, $G_1$ is applied to the first qubit, then $G_2$ is
applied to the second and third qubits, and finally $G_3$ is applied to all
three qubits. The unitary matrix representing this circuit is $G_3 (I \otimes G_2) (G_1 \otimes I \otimes I)$. Notice that we can combine $G_1$
and $G_2$ into the same layer, namely $(I \otimes G_2) (G_1 \otimes I \otimes I) = G_1 \otimes G_2$. The **depth** of a circuit is the
number of layers of gates it has, and in the above example the depth is
$2$.

Next we introduce some common gates. The first such example is the
**controlled-NOT** or **CNOT** gate. It has the following
action:

$$
\left\lvert 00\right\rangle \mapsto \left\lvert 00\right\rangle, \;
\left\lvert 01\right\rangle \mapsto \left\lvert 01\right\rangle, \;
\left\lvert 10\right\rangle \mapsto \left\lvert 11\right\rangle, \;
\left\lvert 11\right\rangle \mapsto \left\lvert 10\right\rangle.
$$

Namely, it maps $\left\lvert xy\right\rangle$ to $\left\lvert xz\right\rangle$ where $z = x \oplus y$.

A related gate is the **Toffoli** gate, or controlled-controlled-NOT
gate, which maps $\left\lvert x\right\rangle \left\lvert y\right\rangle \left\lvert z\right\rangle \mapsto \left\lvert x\right\rangle \left\lvert y\right\rangle \left\lvert z \oplus xy\right\rangle$. For example, we have $\left\lvert 011\right\rangle \mapsto \left\lvert 011\right\rangle$,
but $\left\lvert 110\right\rangle \mapsto \left\lvert 111\right\rangle$. Finally, we also have a **SWAP**
gate, which maps $\left\lvert x\right\rangle \left\lvert y\right\rangle$ to $\left\lvert y\right\rangle \left\lvert x\right\rangle$ for all $x, y \in \lbrace0, 1\rbrace$.

Up to now, none of the gates can be used to create superposition. To
achieve superposition, the canonical choice is the **Hadamard gate**,
which is defined via

$$
H \left\lvert 0\right\rangle = \frac{\left\lvert 0\right\rangle + \left\lvert 1\right\rangle}{\sqrt{2}} := \left\lvert +\right\rangle,
\quad
H \left\lvert 1\right\rangle = \frac{\left\lvert 0\right\rangle - \left\lvert 1\right\rangle}{\sqrt{2}} := \left\lvert -\right\rangle.
$$

It is worth noting that $H^2 = I$.

Notice that all of the gates we have defined so far are real. For gates with
complex entries, we first have a **phase gate** $S$ defined via

$$
S \left\lvert 0\right\rangle = \left\lvert 0\right\rangle,
\quad
S \left\lvert 1\right\rangle = i \left\lvert 1\right\rangle.
$$

Another type of phase gate, most commonly referred to as the $T$ gate, is
the square root of $S$:

$$
T \left\lvert 0\right\rangle = \left\lvert 0\right\rangle,
\quad
T \left\lvert 1\right\rangle = e^{i \frac{\pi}{4}} \left\lvert 1\right\rangle.
$$

To see how a quantum circuit works, one way would be to explicitly compute
the unitary matrix, but it is often more helpful to instead look at how
the system evolves over a basis. This is because a linear map is fully
defined by specifying its behavior on a basis. As an exercise, try to
analyze $(H \otimes H) \operatorname{CNOT} (H \otimes H)$.

## The complexity class $\mathsf{BQP}$

To define $\mathsf{BQP}$, we first need to discuss how a quantum circuit is encoded.
Suppose the circuit is constructed from some universal gate set, where all
the amplitudes used in the gates are efficiently computable. To encode a
quantum circuit $Q$ properly, the encoding bitstring $\left\langle Q \right\rangle$ must
satisfy:

1. The encoding is unique: the mapping from a circuit to its encoding must be injective.
2. The encoding is not too big: if $Q$ has $m$ gates, then $\left\langle Q \right\rangle$ has at most $\operatorname{poly}(m)$ bits.
3. The encoding is not too small: if $Q$ has $m$ gates, then $\left\langle Q \right\rangle$ has at least $m$ bits.

With this, we say a circuit family $\lbrace Q_n \rbrace_{n=1}^\infty$ is **poly-time
uniform** if there exists a poly-time Turing machine such that on input
$1^n$, it outputs $\left\langle Q_n \right\rangle$. Moreover, define **bounded-error
quantum polynomial time ($\mathsf{BQP}$)** as follows: a language $L \in \mathsf{BQP}$ if
there is a poly-time uniform quantum circuit family $\lbrace Q_n \rbrace_{n=1}^\infty$ and a
polynomial $q$ such that for all $x \in \lbrace0, 1\rbrace^n$, $x \in L$ implies the
probability of measuring $\left\lvert 1\right\rangle$ on the first qubit of $Q_n (\left\lvert x\right\rangle \otimes \left\lvert 0^{q(n)}\right\rangle)$ is at least $2/3$, and $x \notin L$ implies the
probability of measuring $\left\lvert 1\right\rangle$ on the first qubit of $Q_n (\left\lvert x\right\rangle \otimes \left\lvert 0^{q(n)}\right\rangle)$ is at most $1/3$. Note that the
$\left\lvert 0^{q(n)}\right\rangle$ states can be seen as the “scratch space” for the
circuit.

How does $\mathsf{BQP}$ compare to other classical complexity classes we know? We
begin by showing $\mathsf{BPP} \subset \mathsf{BQP}$. This can be shown by observing that quantum
circuits can directly simulate AND and NOT gates, which are universal for
classical computation. NOT is already unitary. For AND, simply observe
that applying the Toffoli gate on $\left\lvert x\right\rangle \left\lvert y\right\rangle \left\lvert 0\right\rangle$ gives
$\left\lvert x\right\rangle \left\lvert y\right\rangle \left\lvert x \wedge y\right\rangle$. To “fan-out” a particular state,
we can apply CNOT to $\left\lvert x\right\rangle \left\lvert 0\right\rangle$ to get $\left\lvert x\right\rangle \left\lvert x\right\rangle$. For
the random bits, we can simulate them with $\left\lvert +\right\rangle$. Finally, we can
check that this reduction can be done efficiently in polynomial time, so
$\mathsf{BPP} \subset \mathsf{BQP}$.

For an upper bound, we have $\mathsf{BQP} \subset \mathsf{EXP}$. To prove this, note that
any $n$-qubit state can be represented as a vector in $\mathbb{C}^{2^n}$, and
every quantum gate on an $n$-qubit state is a unitary matrix of size $2^n \times 2^n$. We can compute the state after applying the unitary matrix by
multiplying the matrix and the vector in $O(4^n)$ time. Simulating the
circuit requires at most polynomially many such multiplications, implying
$\mathsf{BQP} \subset \mathsf{EXP}$. This containment showcases what many think to be true
about quantum computation — to classically simulate a general quantum
computer, you need some kind of brute-force exponential-time computation.

In fact, this exponential time algorithm is not space efficient either.
By a theorem we will not prove here, we actually have $\mathsf{BQP} \subset \mathsf{PSPACE}$. The idea is to assume the circuit is constructed from Toffoli
and Hadamard gates, which are computationally universal, and consider the
computation tree induced by the Hadamard gates.
