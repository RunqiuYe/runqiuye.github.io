## Basics of quantum computation

### Qubits

Bits are the basic units of classical computation, and correspondingly **qubits** are their quantum counterparts. A classical bit can be either 0 or 1, but a quantum bit can be in a state of "superposition." When it is measured, there is some probability of observing 0 and some probability of observing 1. We can think of this as a coin flip represented by a vector $v=(v_1,v_2)\in\mathbb{R}^2$, where $v_1$ and $v_2$ are the probabilities of the 0 and 1 outcomes, respectively. For example, the vector $(0.6,0.4)$ gives a probability of $0.6$ of observing 0 and a probability of $0.4$ of observing 1.

In quantum mechanics, however, the state of a qubit is represented by a vector $v=(v_1,v_2)\in\mathbb{C}^2$, where $v_i$ is called the **amplitude** of outcome $i$. For example, in the vector $\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}\right)$, $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$ is the amplitude of the 0 outcome, and $\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}$ is the amplitude of the 1 outcome.

The measurement axiom of quantum mechanics, called the **Born rule**, says that the probability of observing a particular outcome is the squared magnitude of its amplitude. That is, if the state is represented by $v=(v_1,v_2)\in\mathbb{C}^2$, then $\lvert v_1\rvert^2$ is the probability of observing 0, and $\lvert v_2\rvert^2$ is the probability of observing 1. Of course, we must have $\lvert v_1\rvert^2+\lvert v_2\rvert^2=1$. In the example above, the probability of observing either outcome is $1/2$.

We can generalize this description to a quantum state with $d$ outcomes, represented by a complex vector $v\in\mathbb{C}^d$. Again, $\lvert v_i\rvert^2$ is the probability that a measurement produces outcome $i$. For this to be a valid quantum state and for the probabilities to make sense, we must have

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lvert v_i\rvert^2=1.
$$

Equivalently, $\lVert v\rVert_2=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lvert v_i\rvert^2}=1$, where $\lVert\cdot\rVert_2$ is the $\ell_2$-norm on $\mathbb{C}^d$.

### Unitary matrices

Because every valid quantum state must have unit $\ell_2$-norm, viable quantum operations must preserve the $\ell_2$-norm of the state; that is, they must be isometries. An axiom of quantum mechanics also dictates that quantum operations must be **linear**. Recall that norm-preserving linear maps on $\mathbb{C}^d$ are exactly those characterized by **unitary matrices**. These are matrices $U\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}$ such that $UU^\ast=I$, where $U^\ast$ is the conjugate transpose of $U$, and $I$ is the $d\times d$ identity matrix.

### Multi-qubit quantum computation

After understanding single qubits, we would like to understand a quantum system with multiple qubits so that we can build more complicated operations and understand what it means to apply a quantum operation locally.

As a classical analogy, think of two independent coins $A=(0.6,0.4)$ and $B=(0.7,0.3)$. There are four possible outcomes when we flip them: 00, 01, 10, and 11. We can similarly use a vector in $\mathbb{R}^4$ to represent their respective probabilities, namely $(0.42,0.18,0.28,0.12)$.

Combining two independent quantum states works in exactly the same way, and the machinery we use to join them is called the **tensor product**. If $v\in\mathbb{C}^a$ and $w\in\mathbb{C}^b$ represent the quantum states, then the joint system is described by the tensor product $v\otimes w\in\mathbb{C}^{ab}$, where $(v\otimes w)_{ij}=v_iw_j$. We can verify that this vector still has unit $\ell_2$-norm. We can now see that an $n$-qubit state has $2^n$ possible outcomes and is characterized by a vector of dimension $2^n$.

Correspondingly, for quantum operations we have the **tensor product of matrices**. The tensor product of matrices is the unique operator that represents the tensor product of the underlying states. That is, for unitary matrices $U\in\mathbb{C}^{a\times a}$ and $V\in\mathbb{C}^{b\times b}$, the tensor product $U\otimes V$ is defined as the unique linear operator such that

$$
(U\otimes V)(v\otimes w)=(Uv)\otimes(Vw)
$$

for all $v\in\mathbb{C}^a$ and $w\in\mathbb{C}^b$. The intuition is that applying a unitary operation to a specific qubit should not affect any other qubit.

### Dirac notation and inner products

To express quantum states, we use Dirac notation. The most basic notational idea is that we use a **ket**, which looks like $\lvert\cdot\rangle$, to describe a vector that represents a quantum state. For example, we express the 0-outcome and 1-outcome states as

$$
\lvert 0\rangle=
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
0
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\lvert 1\rangle=
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
1
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Any single-qubit state $\lvert\psi\rangle$ can be written as $\lvert\psi\rangle=\alpha\lvert0\rangle+\beta\lvert1\rangle$, where $\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}$ and $\lvert\alpha\rvert^2+\lvert\beta\rvert^2=1$. To express multi-qubit states, for any $x=(x_1,\ldots,x_n)\in\{0,1\}^n$ we write

$$
\lvert x\rangle=\lvert x_1\rangle\otimes\cdots\otimes\lvert x_n\rangle.
$$

These are called **classical basis states**. Indeed, they form a basis in which any $n$-qubit state can be expressed as

$$
\lvert\psi\rangle=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^n}\alpha_x\lvert x\rangle,
$$

where $\alpha_x\in\mathbb{C}$ and $\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^n}\lvert\alpha_x\rvert^2=1$.

In addition to the tensor product, we also have the familiar inner product. In Dirac notation, we use a **bra** to express the conjugate transpose of a ket. If $\lvert\psi\rangle=(\psi_1,\ldots,\psi_d)$ and $\lvert\phi\rangle=(\phi_1,\ldots,\phi_d)$ are states in $\mathbb{C}^d$, then their inner product is written as

$$
\langle\psi\mid\phi\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\psi_i^\ast\phi_i,
$$

where $\psi_i^\ast$ denotes the complex conjugate of $\psi_i$. Their outer product $\lvert\phi\rangle\langle\psi\rvert$ is the matrix given by $\bigl(\lvert\phi\rangle\langle\psi\rvert\bigr)_{ij}=\phi_i\psi_j^\ast$.

An arbitrary $n$-qubit unitary matrix $U$ can now be characterized by

$$
U=\sum_{x,y\in\{0,1\}^n}u_{x,y}\lvert x\rangle\langle y\rvert,
$$

where $u_{x,y}=\langle x\rvert U\lvert y\rangle$. The corresponding linear-algebraic interpretations of these concepts are straightforward, so we leave their verification as an exercise.
